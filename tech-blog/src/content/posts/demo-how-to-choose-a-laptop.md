## Start With Your Use Case, Not the Spec Sheet

The most reliable way to choose a laptop is to work backward from what you'll actually do with it: writing and browsing, spreadsheets and video calls, software development, video/photo editing, or gaming. Each use case has a different bottleneck — and paying for specs you won't use is the most common way to overspend.

## A Simple Evaluation Framework

| Use Case | What Matters Most | What Matters Less |
|---|---|---|
| Browsing, docs, email | Battery life, keyboard, weight | Dedicated GPU |
| Development | RAM, CPU cores, storage speed | GPU (unless ML/graphics work) |
| Video/photo editing | GPU, color-accurate display, storage | Ultra-light weight |
| Gaming | Dedicated GPU, cooling, refresh rate | Battery life |

## Specs Worth Understanding

- **CPU**: Generation and core count matter more than clock speed alone.
- **RAM**: Determines how many applications and browser tabs you can run smoothly at once.
- **Storage**: An SSD (solid-state drive) is meaningfully faster than a traditional hard drive for everyday use.
- **Display**: Resolution and color accuracy matter more for creative work than for general use.
- **Battery**: Manufacturer-quoted battery life is measured under light use — real-world use is typically shorter.

## Build Quality Signals

Specs don't capture everything. Before buying, if possible: try the keyboard and trackpad, check the hinge for wobble, and look at the warranty and support policy — these vary a lot between brands and are hard to judge from a spec sheet alone.

## A Note on Pricing

This guide intentionally does not list prices — they change constantly and vary by region and retailer. Compare the current price against the specs you actually need, not against a number you saw somewhere else.
